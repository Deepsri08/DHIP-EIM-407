2. Problem Clarification

2.1 Significance of colonoscopy

Colonoscopy is a medical procedure used to visually examine the inside of a patient’s lower gastrointestinal tract, mainly the large intestine (colon) and the rectum [1].

In Singapore, adults aged 50 and above are recommended to undergo a colonoscopy once every 10 years to screen for colorectal cancer [2]. It is the gold standard for screening of colorectal cancer and has both diagnostic and therapeutic interventions. Diagnostic interventions include examining the lining of the colon and rectum for signs of inflammation, bleeding and/or extracting a small tissue sample (biopsy) from any areas of suspicion to be analysed in a lab. Besides colorectal cancer, this intervention can also be done to diagnose inflammatory bowel diseases and diverticulosis [3].

Figure 1: Illustration of polypectomy [4]

As for therapeutic interventions, in most cases, a polypectomy of lesions with malignant potential is performed [3]. Polypectomy refers to the procedure executed to surgically remove a polyp, a small growth of tissue on the colon wall. Polyps can be cancerous, non-cancerous or precancerous, where they could turn into cancer in the future. Polyps are removed using forceps or a wire loop called snare, that is positioned at the base of the polyp and tightened to cut it, and remove it.

There are 50 million colonoscopies [5] performed globally every year, with at least 10-12k performed in SGH alone every year [6].

2.2 How is colonoscopy performed?

For a colonoscopy to be effective, first the colon must be empty of any bowel, thus patients will be given laxatives such as PEG solutions to cleanse the colon. Before the procedure, patients will receive moderate sedation to promote relaxation and alleviate discomfort, while maintaining the ability to respond to any stimuli.

Figure 2: Anatomy of Colon [7]

Following the sedation, a 1.65 metre flexible tube, known as a colonoscope, will be used by the endoscopist to advance into the anus. This colonoscope is advanced all the way to the cecum (Fig. 2), the beginning section of the colon, to enable full visualisation of the colon during the procedure.

Figure 3: Picture of a colonoscope [8]

The colonoscope has a camera lens mounted at the tip to transmit images of the colonic mucosa to a computer processing unit, which displays them on a monitor for endoscopists to view. The scope is equipped with two controls to deflect the scope tip vertically (up and down) and horizontally (left and right), each providing approximately 180 degrees of angulation. The scope also has dedicated channels for air and water insufflation, allowing controlled delivery of air and water into the colon to enhance visibility and facilitate the procedure.

2.3 Current standard of care

Colonoscopy is benchmarked by quality indicators such as cecum intubation and adenoma detection rate. Cecum intubation refers to the process of successfully reaching the cecum during colonoscopy and adenoma detection rate refers to the average rate of precancerous polyps that the doctor identifies and removes in each colonoscopy [9].

However, the passage of advancement to the cecum and withdrawal of the scope from the cecum also play a significant role in colonoscopy, affecting the overall time and quality of the procedure [10]. If no challenges are present in these pathways, the optimal time to reach cecum would be two to three minutes (Appendix A) and the withdrawal time would be more than eight minutes [11], adding up to a total of estimated 10 minutes taken for an optimal colonoscopy procedure. However, this is often not the case.

Figure 4: Flowchart of workflow during colonoscopy

During colonoscopy, minimally a junior/mid-career/senior endoscopist and two nurses are required. Patient will be lying on the bed in a lateral position. The endoscopist will insert the colonoscope into the patient by holding the tip of the scope in one hand and rotating all the other knobs with the other hand. The nurses would provide assistance when needed by applying manual abdominal pressure on the patient, facilitate patient repositioning and/or assist with polyp removal.

Figure 5: Bending of shaft causing loop [12]
Figure 6: Table of common loops formed during colonoscopy [13], [14]

During colonoscopy, the colonoscope loops within the colon, 91% of the time [15]. Looping occurs when the shaft of the colonoscope (Figure 3) bends back on itself (Figure 5) as the tip encounters resistance at tight bends, mainly in the sigmoid and transverse region of the colon (Figure 6), hindering forward advancement.

Figure 7: Detailed colon anatomy (mobile segments: sigmoid and transverse) [16]

Looping causes lateral stretching of colon walls, causing extreme discomfort [17] among patients during the procedure. The extent of loop formation varies among individuals and is more pronounced in those with prior abdominal surgeries, extreme BMIs, older age (Appendix B) or female anatomy [18]. Loops are formed mainly at the mobile parts of the colon (Figure 7), that allow space for the scope to buckle and twist in all directions of the axis. This mobility of the colon, coupled with the push force exerted on the colonoscope by the endoscopist, translates to poor transmission of force to the tip of the scope, creating a paradoxical movement (Appendix B) that prevents scope advancement.

During the procedure, both the endoscopists and nurses play a key role in preventing loop formation, contributing to increased demands of nurse workload and reducing these loops formed puts a high taskload on endoscopists. As illustrated in Figure 4, loop reduction (Appendix B) is performed by the endoscopist torquing the scope clockwise, withdrawing it slightly, and using air or water insufflation techniques to distend and stiffen the colon. Nurses step in to apply abdominal pressure (Appendix B.1), under the endoscopist's guidance, to prevent re-looping as the scope advances further. Abdominal pressure can only be applied after the determination of the location of loop formation in the patient, often subject to trial and error and limited by hand surface area and consistency of pressure application. In worst cases, patient repositioning has to be performed, and if the patient is heavier, more staff would be needed. If all attempts fail, a repeat colonoscopy might be scheduled.

2.4 Impact of looping during colonoscopy

Looping during colonoscopy prolongs procedural time as additional time is required to reach the cecum, extending up to 2 hours compared to the average 30 minutes (Appendix A). This increases risk of undetected adenomas if cecal intubation is incomplete and raises patient discomfort and sedation-related complications. Looping also significantly increases task load on endoscopists and nursing staff, with 70.1% of endoscopists and 87.8% nurses reporting work-related injuries due to physical demands involved [19]. Given that looping occurs 91% of the time during a colonoscopy and leads to significant complications, addressing this issue has an enormous potential to improve the outcomes for routinely performed colonoscopy.

3. Market Analysis of Existing solutions

Existing market solutions claiming to address loop formation during colonoscopy were systematically reviewed for their approach and outcomes.


Figure 8: Competitor Analysis

In Singapore, only ScopeGuide is primarily used to assist with loops formed during colonoscopy. However, it has a limited presence (only two units in SGH) and its cumbersome setup restricts widespread usage. Moreover, it only visualizes loop types without providing relative location to anatomical positioning, which is crucial for effective loop reduction (Appendix B.2).

Exploring international markets, robotic assistive devices like Neoguide, although effective, face high costs. Aer-o-scope lacks channels for therapeutic intervention, which limits clinical workflow integration. Pathfinder involves additional steps of adding a sleeve to the current scope to add rigidity, and Colowrap is only effective for a specific patient group. These limitations highlight a significant market gap for a solution that is user-friendly, easily adoptable into clinical workflow, and effective in reducing loop formation during colonoscopy.

4. Value proposition

A cost-effective solution with ≤ 5 minute set-up time to reduce endoscope looping during colonoscopy in adult patients, to:

  1. Decrease insertion time to cecum by ≥ 15% per colonoscopy
  2. Reduce task load for junior / mid-career endoscopists (mental demand, physical demand and frustration) by ≥ 20%
  3. Reduce nurse workload (manual abdominal compression time, numbers of patient repositioning) by ≥ 50%

Indirectly, the shorter procedural time will also benefit patients through reduced sedation needs and discomfort caused by loops.

5. Design methodology

The goal is to create a cost-effective device that can be easily integrated into the clinical workflow to reduce the occurrence of looping during colonoscopy. With such a device we aim to reduce cecum intubation time, reduce taskload for junior and mid-career endoscopists, and reduce physical workload for nurses. 6.2 Detailed final concept design

Since abdominal pressure device had the highest average score that met the needs criteria as well as the lowest risk based on the risk matrix, abdominal pressure device was chosen as the final concept.

Figure 15: Abdominal pressure device

Abdominal pressure device involves three components. First being the binder that would be applied onto the patient, with a disposable plastic lining to ensure sterility as well as reusability of the component. Secondly, there is a tightening mechanism that ensures the generation of inward radial pressure onto the next component from the tension from the binder. The last component, most significant, applies the effective pressure onto the patients’ abdomen to splint the mobile segments of the colon to apply the counterforce required to prevent loop formation during colonoscopy.

7. Prototyping

With the final concept selected, we developed a prototype plan to identify key questions/ risks for efficacy testing (Appendix C).

The primary focus was to determine optimal location and direction for effective pressure application and validate whether the device could reliably replicate such parameters. Objective was to target common N-loops forming in the sigmoid region, for the purpose of prototyping.

(A)

15cm laterally

Figure 16: Parameters [22], [23]

We determined that the optimal direction and location for effective abdominal pressure was an inferolateral direction, estimated at 45°, 15cm laterally from the umbilicus to the Anterior Superior Iliac Spine (ASIS) (Appendix C, [22]).

7.1 Solid block model

Question to answer:
We wanted to simulate force in an interforal direction on the abdomen, using a solid-state mechanical compression system.

Figure 17: Prototype 1

We used a soft neoprene abdominal binder and attached it to polystyrene styrofoam blocks of different geometries to visualise force transmission. Variables such as material, volume of block, type of abdominal binder, relative position of abdominal binder to block was kept constant. This experiment was conducted on a colon model to replicate the mobile sigmoid region, and silica beads wrapped in ziplock bags were used to mimic abdominal tissue.

Outcome

Abdominal binder provided a 90° inward force on the silica beads and the angled blocks contributed to only an inward and downward force; however, there was no lateral direction towards the ASIS. Regardless of geometry, foam block compression did not translate in the interforal direction.

7.2 Solid block with pneumatic system model

Question to answer:
With the same question to answer, we brainstormed to add a dynamic system to simulate an interforal direction, which led to the addition of the pneumatic system.

Figure 18: Prototype 2

Same experiment was repeated with the addition of an air bladder attached to the bottom of the styrofoam block, connected to an inflation bulb.

Outcome

The intended interforal direction was achieved; however clinical mentors raised concerns that styrofoam (solid compression) may be too bulky and cause patient discomfort. Additionally, the angled placement of the air bladder beneath the styrofoam resulted in point pressure application, which was ineffective. This highlighted the need to determine the sufficient contact surface area to ensure effective pressure distribution.

7.3 Pneumatics system model

Question to answer:
With this next iteration, we aimed to ensure patient comfort and determine optimal surface area of effective pressure needed in the interforal direction on the abdomen.

Figure 19: Prototype 3

The styrofoam block was replaced with a plastic trapezoidal prism shaped holder attached to the air bladder, large enough to cover the whole sigmoid region. This was attached beneath the same abdominal binder set-up.

Outcome

With quick testing, air bladder seemed to expand upwards due to insufficient compression from the abdominal binder.

About pediatric colonoscopes

Pediatric colonoscopes have a smaller diameter of 10 to 11mm, compared to adult colonoscopes, which are 13mm in diameter. They are better suited for patients with previous adhesions from surgeries or diverticular disease, as they allow for greater flexibility to maneuver around the fixed and tightly angulated colons. The downside to this is the potential of this scope to cause an excessive number of loops due to more movement, which may increase procedural time as these loops have to be resolved before the scope can be progressed further into the colon.

Why sigmoid loops the most? The sigmoid colon length can vary considerably, ranging from 40 to 70 cm when stretched during scope insertion and crumple down to only 30–35 cm when the scope is straightened fully. This makes precise anatomical location within the sigmoid by virtue of distance on insertion unreliable. The sigmoid colon mesentery is inserted in a V shape across the pelvic brim, which can be modified considerably by adhesions from previous inflammatory processes or abdominopelvic surgery. The pelvic brim and the forward-projecting sacral promontory, causes the colonoscope to pass anteriorly before passing posteriorly again toward the left paracolic gutter. This anatomical configuration in more than 80% of cases will cause the colonoscope to take a clockwise anteroposterior spiral into the descending colon. Loop resolution techniques will need to be undertaken by the colonoscopists to facilitate smooth and safe scope insertion and to minimize patient discomfort.

Appendix B.1

Abdominal pressure

Common practice of abominable compression:

Apply pressure using the palm or forearm in a specific direction, mostly downwards onto the abdomen area where the colonoscope is suspected by the endoscopist or the “feel” of the scope by the nurse.

Techniques:

  1. Open hand technique with fingers spread
  2. Prechel pressure - using forearm as a splint with body weight providing downward pressure, focus on one directional force

Ergonomic consideration to avoid wrist injury and to allow body mechanics to deliver stable pressure, hence unable to apply multidirectional forces.

Applying pressure at the optimal position (e.g., sigmoid colon for common loops) helps stabilize the colon and facilitates smoother passage of the endoscope, minimizing resistance, discomfort, and risk of injury. The actual force needed is usually just enough to prevent movement or reduce the loop; excessive force does not improve outcomes and may cause harm.

Short scope technique done in Japan

First developed by Professor Shin-ei Kudo and widely recognized among endoscopists in Japan as the most ideal colonic intubation method. Knowledge of this technique remains limited outside of Japan. The focus is how to reach the descending colon without extending the rectum and sigmoid colon. If the descending colon is intubated with the technique, the rest of the large colon will be more easily accessible by continuing with the technique.

Appendix B.4

Concept Solutions
  1. Convertible rigidity wire
    A wire inserted into the working accessory channel of the current colonoscope to increase rigidity of the scope when needed.
  2. Endovine sleeve
    Mirroring parallels to a vine robot system, where air will be supplied to the sleeve and propel the scope tip forward by push force.
  3. Silicone sleeve propeller
    A sleeve over the endoscope that exerts extra force just at the tip through air pressure to pull the scope tip.
  4. Inflatable guide
    Allows the inflation and deflation of the balloon attached to the scope. Can be inflated to match the diameter of the colon, to splint the colon in place while allowing the scope to advance forward.

Optimal compressional direction

Sigmoid colon often loops anteriorly and lies near the left lower abdomen, applying pressure downward and inward toward the pelvis can reduce “size of loop” by buffering the colon against movement, helping with scope advancement.

Compression directed from umbilicus to left lower quadrant is recommended to prevent loop as the scope advances through the descending colon, consistent with the anatomy, typical position of the sigmoid and mobility supported by mesentery allowing it to loop.
